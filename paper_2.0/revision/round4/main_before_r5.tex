\title{In Llama, Saying ``I Don't Know'' Tracks\\Entity Popularity More Than Knowledge}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We investigate what a language model's \idk{} tracks: whether the model lacks the fact, or how familiar the entity is. We cross entity popularity (Wikipedia page views in PopQA) with knowledge labels taken from the model's own sampled answers. We find that in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, saying \idk{} tracks popularity more than knowledge. On obscure entities, Llama says \idk{} to \tcObsKnownAbsPct{} of questions it answers correctly in at least 9 of 10 forced samples; on popular entities the rate is \tcPopKnownAbsPct, although its answer entropy is similar in the two cases. The gap holds with knowledge relabeled from a second prompt, with unknown facts regraded by a larger model, and when only replies that name no answer count. Gemma-2-9B rarely abstains under a plain prompt. When the prompt permits \idk, however, its abstention also tracks popularity more than knowledge. As a control, answer entropy follows knowledge, as the labeling implies. Steering and logit-lens readouts do not isolate a familiarity mechanism, so our evidence shows what abstention tracks, not why.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Saying \idk{} when a fact is missing avoids the worst hallucinations \citep{kalai2025hallucinate}. Confidence estimates usually read uncertainty over answers \citep{kadavath2022know,kuhn2023semantic,stolfo2024confidence}, and models use such confidence when a prompt offers abstention \citep{kumaran2026confidence}. However, verbal and answer-level uncertainty diverge \citep{ji2025hedging}, so what a model's \idk{} actually tracks is less clear.

One candidate is familiarity. In people, the sense of being able to answer depends on how familiar a question's terms are as well as on whether the answer can be retrieved \citep{reder1992,schwartz1992,koriat2001cascade}. In language models, ``known entity'' features suppress a default ``can't answer'' response. \citet{lindsey2025biology} propose that a familiar name can trigger these features when the fact is missing, so the model answers when it should abstain. We call this a \emph{misfire}. The converse failure is an unfamiliar name that does not suppress the default: the model then abstains on a fact it knows.

Testing this requires separating familiarity from knowledge. Existing tests define an entity as known when the model answers about it correctly \citep{ferrando2025iknow}, so the two move together. Familiarity has been separated from factual reliability in representations \citep{brzezinka2026bielik} and shown to shape hallucination through fine-tuning data \citep{kang2025unfamiliar}. It has not been crossed with knowledge in abstention behavior; this crossing is what is new here.

We take questions about obscure and popular entities from PopQA \citep{mallen2023popqa} and label each fact known or unknown from the model's own answers \citep{gekhman2024slick}. If abstention follows familiarity, two cells show it: popular unknown facts should draw few \idk{} replies (misfires), and obscure known facts many (the default not suppressed). In Llama, they do (\cref{fig:dissociation}, \cref{tab:cells}). This also bears on representational convergence \citep{huh2024platonic}: do models that agree on answer uncertainty also agree on what makes them abstain? Only Llama and, under a permissive prompt, Gemma let us ask.

\paragraph{Main Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Popularity Over Knowledge in Llama:} Llama's popularity preference is \hmLlamaDiff{} \hmLlamaDiffCI{} (\cref{sec:main}). It survives four label and measurement checks.
\item \textbf{Same Preference in Gemma When Permitted:} under a permissive prompt, Gemma's preference is \pmGemmaDiff{} \pmGemmaDiffCI{} (\cref{sec:families}). Under the plain prompt it rarely abstains.
\item \textbf{No Isolated Familiarity Mechanism:} steering moves a generic abstention signal, and a base-model lens responds to both factors (\cref{sec:mech}).
\end{enumerate}
Together, these findings mean that, in this extreme-contrast design, Llama's answer uncertainty alone predicts its abstention poorly.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Questions \& Labels.} We use the 16 relations of PopQA. Within each relation, subjects in the bottom and top deciles of Wikipedia page views (a proxy for familiarity) form the \emph{obscure} and \emph{popular} arms. For each model, we grade a greedy answer and ten samples ($T=0.7$) against gold aliases, under a \emph{forced-answer prompt} (the chat template plus the assistant prefix ``The answer is''). A fact is \emph{known} if the greedy answer and at least nine samples are correct, and \emph{unknown} if no sample is; the band between is dropped. This leaves \faLlamaN, \faQwenN, \faGemmaN{} and \faMistralN{} questions for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B-it and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \citep{llama3,qwen25,gemma2,mistral7b} (cell counts in \cref{app:robust}).

\paragraph{Two Readouts.} \emph{Answer uncertainty} is the first-token entropy under the forced-answer prompt. The knowledge labels come from the same distribution, so entropy should follow knowledge; it serves as a control, and we also relabel knowledge from an independent prompt. \emph{Abstention} is measured with a \emph{free prompt} (the chat template alone): for up to 100 questions per popularity $\times$ knowledge cell (\hmLlamaItems{} in total), we draw three replies ($T=0.7$, up to 96 tokens). Two LLM judges (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, Gemma-2-27B-it) label each reply DONT\_KNOW, CLARIFY (asks which entity is meant) or ANSWER. A reply abstains when both say DONT\_KNOW. On Llama the judges agree at $\kappa=\hmLlamaKappa$ \lkKappaCI, above a pre-specified bar of \kappaBar; on the other families they do not (\cref{sec:families}).

\paragraph{Effects.} The popularity effect is the obscure-minus-popular difference in a readout, averaged over the two knowledge levels. The knowledge effect is the unknown-minus-known difference, averaged over popularity. Their difference (popularity effect minus knowledge effect) is the model's \emph{popularity preference}: positive when abstention follows popularity more than knowledge. In this balanced $2\times2$, it equals abstention on obscure known facts minus abstention on popular unknown facts. Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps over questions within cells, not clustered by relation. Unless marked post hoc, every analysis followed a plan written and hashed before it ran (\emph{pre-specified}; \cref{app:timing}).

\paragraph{Label \& Measurement Checks: The Preference Survives.} Relabeling knowledge with a paraphrased question and prefix agrees with the original labels at $\kappa=\lcKappa$. Of the \gsObsKnownTotal{} obscure known facts, \eoObsKnownN{} stay known; these abstain at \eoObsKnown. The preference is \eoDiff{} \eoDiffCI, and entropy still follows knowledge. Popular entities may have valid answers outside PopQA's aliases. We therefore had Llama-3.3-70B regrade forced answers by meaning. It found \sePUKnown{} of the 100 popular unknown facts known and \sePUInter{} in between; with its labels, the preference is \seDiff{} \seDiffCI. The \idk{} replies are refusals, not hedged answers. With Llama-3.3-70B as a third judge and a majority rule, $\kappa=\tjLlamaKappa$ and the preference is \tjLlamaDiff. At most \lcDKAliasMax{} of the \idk{} replies in any cell contain the gold answer (post hoc). Counting only replies that the 70B judge labels as declining without naming any candidate gives \sdDiff{} \sdDiffCI.

\paragraph{Recognition \& Ambiguity: Neither Explains It.} Llama describes the entity correctly in \ekObsKnown{} of its completions for obscure known facts and \ekPopKnown{} for popular known ones. Adding this LLM-graded recognition score to a regression shrinks the popularity coefficient by \ekShrink. If obscure names were ambiguous, Llama might hedge for that reason. The data point the other way (post hoc): popular names are shared with more Wikidata entities (median \amPopKnownHom--\amPopUnkHom, against \amObsKnownHom{} for obscure known facts), and obscure known facts with a unique name still draw \idk{} in \amSingle{} of replies. Name spelling explains part of the gap between matched pairs of known facts, but barely changes the popularity coefficient over all questions (post hoc; \cref{app:robust}).

\paragraph{Familiarity: Not Shown Directly.} Neither model-side measure shows the obscure names to be unfamiliar to Llama: name surprisal is uncorrelated with page views (\cref{app:surprisal}), and recognition is higher for obscure known facts. The result has the shape the misfire idea predicts, but it does not establish the mechanism.

\paragraph{Within One Prompt: The Split Remains.} At the forced-answer position, the first token's lean toward hedge tokens (refusal openers such as `` I'', `` not'', `` unknown'') follows popularity and knowledge equally (\loLlamaDiff{} \loLlamaDiffCI; \cref{app:families}). After seeing this, we specified a reading at the free prompt, where the log-odds of a first token ``I'' follow popularity (\eoFreeIPop{} vs.\ \eoFreeIKnow), while its entropy follows knowledge (\eoFreeEntKnow{} vs.\ \eoFreeEntPop{} nats).

\subsection{Other Families: Gemma Tracks Popularity When Permitted}
\label{sec:families}

\paragraph{Plain Prompt: Too Few Abstentions to Measure.} Entropy follows knowledge in all four families (\cref{fig:dissociation}c), but Qwen, Gemma and Mistral rarely say \idk, and the judges disagree on them. With a third judge, Gemma reaches $\kappa=\tjGemmaKappa$ but abstains in at most \tjGemmaObsUnk{} of replies in any cell, so its preference cannot be estimated. A pre-specified four-family test on the forced-answer hedge readout found both of its conditions in Gemma and one in Qwen and Mistral, but that readout shows no preference in Llama either (\cref{app:families}).

\paragraph{Permissive Prompt: Same Preference in Gemma.} We then ended the question with ``If you do not know the answer, say that you do not know''. Both models abstain often, and three judges agree ($\kappa=\pmLlamaKappa$, \pmGemmaKappa). The popularity preference is \pmLlamaDiff{} \pmLlamaDiffCI{} for Llama and \pmGemmaDiff{} \pmGemmaDiffCI{} for Gemma. Gemma says \idk{} to \pmGemmaObsKnown{} of obscure known questions and \pmGemmaPopKnown{} of popular known ones. Cross-model agreement on that readout, beyond shared labels, met a post hoc rule for one of three pairs (\cref{app:crossmodel}).

\subsection{Mechanistic Tests: No Familiarity Cause Isolated}
\label{sec:mech}

\paragraph{Steering: A Generic Abstention Signal.} Like work steering refusal along recognition directions \citep{ferrando2025iknow,brzezinka2026familiarity}, we fitted three directions on half of the questions \citep{arditi2024refusal,rimsky2024caa}: obscure-minus-popular, unknown-minus-known and abstain-minus-answer. We added each at a multiple (the dose) of the obscure-minus-popular direction's norm, chose the dose on a second set and scored a third (\cref{app:steer}). Obscure-minus-popular raised Llama's abstention from \svLlamaNone{} to \svLlamaFam, \svLlamaDOne{} \svLlamaDOneCI{} over random directions (short of the pre-specified $+0.10$ bar), and Gemma's from \svGemmaNone{} to \svGemmaFam{} at \svGemmaDose$\times$ the norm (\cref{fig:steering}a). In Llama, the direction is nearly parallel to the abstention direction (cosine \svLlamaCos), partly by construction, since abstaining questions are mostly obscure. Removing that component leaves \svLlamaDTwo{} \svLlamaDTwoCI{} (Gemma \svGemmaDTwo{} \svGemmaDTwoCI). Fitted on obscure questions alone, the abstention direction still has cosine \wlLlamaCos, and removing it leaves \wlLlamaDTwo{} \wlLlamaDTwoCI{} (three judges). Obscure-minus-popular beats unknown-minus-known on abstention (\svLlamaDThree{} \svLlamaDThreeCI), as the cells' rates predict. In Gemma, unknown-minus-known raises entropy \svGemmaDFour{} \svGemmaDFourCI{} nats more (\cref{fig:steering}b; cf.\ \citealp{che2026steering}).

\paragraph{Lens: Both Factors in Base Models.} With prefitted Jacobian lenses \citep{gurnee2026workspace}, read at the forced-answer position in a pre-specified mid-depth window, popularity raises the hedge-token term in Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-2-9B, Gemma-2-2B and OLMo-3-7B \citep{olmo3}. Knowledge raises it about as much (\cref{app:lens}, \cref{fig:lens}). Instruct partners lean further toward knowledge, so this readout does not show where Llama-Instruct's preference arises.

\section{Limitations}

Abstention is judged by LLMs not validated against human labels. The design uses only the extreme page-view deciles, facts answered correctly at least nine times in ten or never, and cells of at most 100 questions; models have 2--9B parameters, and questions name a single entity. Hedging driven by ambiguity that the judges do not label as clarification is not excluded, and whether other families share the preference is open.

\emph{Design counts.} Llama's pool has \lkObsPoolKnown{} known, \lkObsPoolMaybe{} intermediate and \lkObsPoolUnk{} unknown obscure facts, and \lkPopPoolKnown{}, \lkPopPoolMaybe{} and \lkPopPoolUnk{} popular ones; the two-judge sample is a stratified random sample of 100 questions per cell (seed 0), or all of a smaller cell. Intervals for differences of cell means use 10,000 within-cell bootstraps (seed 0); linear probability models use 2,000 bootstraps over questions.

\emph{Measurements.} The single corrected judge gives the smallest difference. Its 24-token replies are often cut off before the model commits; why it and the regex disagree on the same replies is not established. The two-judge measurement with 96 tokens was designed for that reason after the \hjLlamaDiff{} was seen and made primary before it ran. Regex recall on the 96-token replies, against the two-judge labels, is \hmLlamaRegexRecall. Each judge's agreement with the three-judge majority: Qwen \lkMajQwen, Gemma \lkMajGemma, Llama-3.3-70B \lkMajLlama.

\emph{Independent labels} (pre-specified). Knowledge was relabeled with one paraphrased question per relation, the assistant prefix ``Answer:'' and fresh samples, under the same rule. Of the \lcBothN{} questions labeled under both prompts, \lcKK{} are known under both, \lcUU{} unknown under both, \lcKU{} known only originally and \lcUK{} only under the paraphrase (agreement \lcAgree, $\kappa=\lcKappa$); most other changes move popular known facts to the dropped band. Where both labelings agree, cells have \lcConsN{} questions (obscure unknown, obscure known, popular unknown, popular known). Under the new labels, the popularity effect on abstention is \eoPop{} \eoPopCI{} and the knowledge effect \eoKnow{} \eoKnowCI, and original-prompt entropy follows them (knowledge minus popularity \eoEntKmP{} \eoEntKmPCI).

\emph{Regraded unknown facts} (pre-specified). Llama-3.3-70B graded each of a fresh greedy and ten sampled forced answers for every unknown fact as naming the gold entity or not. Of the 100 popular unknown facts in the sample, \sePUStay{} stay unknown, \sePUKnown{} become known and \sePUInter{} intermediate (dropped); of the 100 obscure unknown facts, \seOUStay{}, \seOUKnown{} and \seOUInter. Dropping instead every popular unknown question with a gold alias in any free reply (\lcPUAlias{} of them) gives \lcAliasDrop{} \lcAliasDropCI.

\emph{Strict declines} (pre-specified). The 70B judge labeled each reply as declining without naming a candidate, hedging with a candidate, answering, or asking which entity is meant. Declines occur in \sdObsKnown{} of obscure known replies and \sdPopKnown{} of popular known ones; among replies both judges call DONT\_KNOW, at most \sdHedgedMax{} per cell are hedged answers.

\emph{Entity recognition} (pre-specified). Llama completed ``\{subject\} is'' after the question ``What is \{subject\}?'' (greedy and five samples), and Llama-3.3-70B graded each completion against the subject's English Wikidata description; recognition is the share of completions graded correct. Its correlation with log page views over all graded questions is $\rho=\ekRho$. In the linear probability model its coefficient is \ekCoef{} \ekCoefCI{} per standard deviation, and it shrinks the popularity coefficient by \ekShrink{} on the two-judge measure and \ekShrinkRegex{} on the regex measure.

\emph{Guessable answers} (post hoc). An answer counts as guessable if a gold alias of at least three characters appears in the subject's name, or if it is its relation's most common answer and covers at least 20\% of the relation's pool. Among unknown facts alone the popularity gap in abstention is \hmLlamaPopUnkOnly{} \hmLlamaPopUnkOnlyCI. On the regex measure over the \gsRegexN{} non-guessable questions the difference is \gsRegexDiff{} \gsRegexDiffCI.

\emph{Namesakes} (post hoc). For each subject we counted Wikidata entities with the same English label; medians are \amObsKnownHom{} (obscure known), \amObsUnkHom{} (obscure unknown), \amPopKnownHom{} (popular known) and \amPopUnkHom{} (popular unknown). The covariate is the standardized log count. The \amSingleN{} obscure known facts with a unique name abstain at \amSingle.

\emph{Free replies} (post hoc). In the obscure known cell, \rcObsKnownCorrect{} of free replies contain a gold alias, and \rcObsKnownNonAbsCorrect{} of the replies that do not abstain; for \rcBothN{} of \rcBothOf{} questions, one free reply gives the correct answer and another says \idk. A linear probability model of abstention gives popularity \rcPopEnt{} \rcPopEntCI, knowledge \rcKnowEnt{} \rcKnowEntCI{} and \rcEntCoef{} per standard deviation of forced-answer entropy.

\emph{Name spelling} (post hoc). Between \scPopKnownPairs{} matched pairs of known facts, one obscure and one popular, the regex abstention gap of \scPopKnownGap{} shrinks to \scPopKnownGapSurface{} \scPopKnownGapSurfaceCI{} once the names' word frequency, token count and token rarity are held equal. Over the \faLlamaN{} labeled questions, the popularity coefficient on the regex measure moves from \scPoolPop{} to \scPoolPopSurface{} logits per standard deviation after adding the name's word frequency, token count and token rarity. The matched pairs compare only known facts, one popular with one obscure fact of similar surface form, which is why they lose more of the gap.

\section{Steering details}
\label{app:steer}

Pre-specified (\cref{app:timing}). Directions were fitted on a random half of each model's questions at layer round$(0.55\times(L-1))$, the last position of the free prompt: obscure minus popular and unknown minus known (differences of cell means, each averaged over the other factor), and abstain minus answer (questions whose stored free-generation regex abstention rate is at least 0.5 minus those with rate 0). Their natural norms are \svLlamaNormFam, \svLlamaNormKnow{} and \svLlamaNormAbst{} in Llama and \svGemmaNormFam, \svGemmaNormKnow{} and \svGemmaNormAbst{} in Gemma. The obscure-minus-popular direction with its abstention component projected out was rescaled to the original norm. Each direction was added at a dose $k\in\{0.5,1,2,4,8\}$ times the obscure-minus-popular direction's natural norm, at the last prompt position and every generated position. $k$ was chosen on 150 popular known questions as the smallest value at which that direction beat five random directions by 0.10 in regex abstention, a rule separate from the confirmatory bar (\svLlamaDose$\times$ for Llama, \svGemmaDose$\times$ for Gemma, whose obscure-minus-popular regex rate was \svGemmaSelOne{} at 1$\times$ and \svGemmaSelTwo{} at 2$\times$). All conditions were scored on 150 further popular known questions, \lkSteerOverlap{} of which are also in the two-judge sample.

\section{Lens: window effects, the instruct models and onsets}
\label{app:lens}

Pre-specified. Hedge term: log-sum-exp of the lens log-probabilities of the hedge tokens `` not'', `` I'', `` unknown'', `` unclear'' and `` no''; the full readout subtracts the same quantity for the answer-opening tokens `` a'', `` the'', `` that'', `` in'', `` Yes'' and `` yes''. Window: relative depth $\ell/(L-1)\in[1/3,2/3]$. Intervals: 2,000 within-cell bootstraps. Each model was labeled known or unknown on its own prompt with the same sampling rule.

\emph{Onsets.} The onset of an effect curve is the first layer where it reaches half its positive maximum over layers and its 95\% interval excludes zero. \section{A model-side familiarity measure (negative result)}
\label{app:surprisal}

Pre-specified. We computed the mean per-token surprisal of each subject name under the base Llama-3.1-8B in the context ``Here is a name:''. It is uncorrelated with log page views (Spearman \esRho). With knowledge and name length held fixed, its coefficient per standard deviation is \esJudge{} \esJudgeCI{} on two-judge abstention and \esRegex{} \esRegexCI{} on the regex measure; adding the popularity arm moves the regex coefficient to \esRegexPop{} \esRegexPopCI, although surprisal hardly correlates with the arm (point-biserial \esArmR; \esArmPartial{} given relation and knowledge), which we have not resolved. Surprisal therefore does not stand in for popularity. Short names made of common words (``New York'' as a book title) are a likely reason.
