The central dissociation comes from how facts are labelled, not from anything found in the model. A fact is "known" when the greedy answer and at least 90% of T = 0.7 samples under the forced-answer prompt are correct (lines 41-44). Answer uncertainty is the first-token entropy of that same forced-answer distribution (lines 47-50). A fact whose sampled answers almost never vary must have low entropy, so "entropy follows knowledge" in every family (lines 76-78, Figure 1c) largely restates the selection rule. The other half of the contrast fails for the opposite reason. For obscure subjects, a consistent correct answer under forcing is not evidence of a stored fact. Mallen et al. [2023, Sec. 4.2], the source of the dataset, report that several PopQA relations (country, sport, color) can be guessed from the most frequent answer or from surface cues in the subject's name. The authors admit names that contain their answer (line 417) but test this only on 55 matched pairs (Appendix A), never in the 2,303-question analysis. The regression in lines 79-82 controls for spelling, not for whether the answer can be guessed. On this reading, Llama saying "I don't know" for 0.43 of obscure "known" facts (line 71) correctly reports that it lacks the knowledge, and the +0.39 "popularity effect" measures labelling error. That result rests on one model, 387 questions, and judges never checked against human labels (lines 59-62). The title's two-variable claim is not established.
