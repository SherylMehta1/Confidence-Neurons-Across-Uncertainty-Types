    In Llama, Saying "I Don't Know" Tracks
    Entity Popularity More Than Knowledge

    Abstract

1   When a language model answers a factual question with "I don't know",

2   is it reporting uncertainty about the answer? We cross entity popularity

3   in PopQA with knowledge labels taken from the model's own sampled

4   answers. In Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, abstention follows popularity more than

5   knowledge: about obscure entities the model says "I don't know" in 0.43 of

6   replies even for facts it answers correctly when made to answer, against 0.00

7   for popular entities. This holds with knowledge relabelled from a different

8   prompt, with a third judge, without guessable answers, and controlling

9   for what the model knows about the entity. First-token answer entropy

10  instead follows knowledge, in four model families and under the independent

11  labels; within one prompt, the first token's lean towards "I don't know"

12  follows popularity while its entropy follows knowledge. Mechanistic tests

13  narrow the picture: steering along an obscure-minus-popular direction adds

14  little abstention, and only through its abstention component; base models'

15  decoded hedge readout responds to popularity but as much to knowledge.

16  Gemma-2-9B, the only other family whose abstention we could measure,

17  shows no popularity preference.

1 18 Introduction

19 A model that says "I don't know" when it lacks a fact avoids the worst hallucinations [Kalai
20 et al., 2025]. Confidence estimates usually read uncertainty over answers [Kadavath et al.,
21 2022, Kuhn et al., 2023, Stolfo et al., 2024], and models use such confidence when the prompt
22 invites them to abstain [Kumaran et al., 2026]. Verbal and answer-level uncertainty are
23 known to diverge [Ji et al., 2025]; what each follows, and whether different models follow the
24 same variable, is less clear.

25 People's sense that they can answer a question follows how familiar its terms are, not whether
26 the answer is stored [Reder and Ritter, 1992, Schwartz and Metcalfe, 1992]. In language
27 models, "known entity" features suppress a default "can't answer" response, and Lindsey
28 et al. [2025] propose that a familiar name can trigger them without the fact. Tests of this
29 idea define an entity as known when the model answers about it correctly [Ferrando et al.,
30 2025], so familiarity and knowledge move together.

31 We separate them. From PopQA [Mallen et al., 2023] we take questions about obscure and
32 popular entities and label each known or unknown from the model's own answers [Gekhman
33 et al., 2024]. The deciding observation is the cell where the two disagree: facts about obscure
34 entities that the model answers correctly every time it is made to answer. Our contribution is
35 this factorial test of the misfire idea, its robustness checks, and a comparison across families

2 38 Setup

39 Questions and labels. We use the 16 relations of PopQA. Within each relation, subjects
40 in the bottom and top deciles of Wikipedia page views form the obscure and popular arms;
41 page-view popularity is a proxy for familiarity, and we report it as popularity. For each model,
42 a greedy answer and ten samples (T = 0.7) under a forced-answer prompt (the chat template
43 plus the assistant prefix "The answer is") are graded against gold aliases: a fact is known if
44 the greedy answer and at least 90% of samples are correct and unknown if none is; the rest
45 are dropped. This leaves 2,303, 2,392, 2,282 and 2,378 questions for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct,
46 Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B-it and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [Grattafiori et al., 2024,
47 Qwen et al., 2024, Team et al., 2024, Jiang et al., 2023].

48 Two readouts. Answer uncertainty is the first-token entropy under the forced-answer
49 prompt, whose next token starts an answer. Because the knowledge labels are sampled from
50 the same distribution, entropy is expected to follow knowledge, so we also relabel knowledge
51 from an independent prompt (Section 3.1). Abstention needs the option to decline, so it is
52 measured with a free prompt, the chat template alone: for up to 100 questions per popularity
53 × knowledge cell (387 in total) we draw three replies (T = 0.7, up to 96 tokens). Two
54 LLM judges (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, Gemma-2-27B-it) label each reply DONT_KNOW,
55 CLARIFY (asks which entity is meant) or ANSWER, and a reply abstains when both say
56 DONT_KNOW. On Llama the judges agree at  = 0.62, above a pre-set bar of 0.60; on the
57 other families they do not (Section 3.2). No human validated the judges.

58 Effects. The popularity effect is the obscure-minus-popular difference in a readout averaged
59 over the two knowledge levels, and the knowledge effect the unknown-minus-known difference
60 averaged over popularity, with 95% item-bootstrap intervals. Confirmatory analyses followed
61 criterion notes hashed before each run (Appendix H); the rest are post hoc.

89 3.2 Across families: shared answer uncertainty, different abstention

90 The entropy pattern recurs in all four families (Figure 1c); abstention does not. Qwen,
91 Gemma and Mistral rarely say "I don't know" and the two judges disagree on them; with a
92 third judge, Gemma reaches  = 0.64 but its abstention shows no preference for popularity
93 over knowledge (difference +0.01 [-0.03, +0.06]; at most 0.11 even for obscure, unknown
94 facts), while Qwen ( = 0.41) and Mistral ( = 0.54) remain unmeasured (Appendix B).
95 The popularity preference is thus Llama's, not general.

96 3.3 Mechanistic tests narrow the reading

97 Steering. Earlier work steers refusal along recognition directions [Ferrando et al., 2025,
98 Brzezinka, 2026]. We fitted obscure-minus-popular, unknown-minus-known and abstain-
99 minus-answer directions on half of the questions [Arditi et al., 2024, Rimsky et al., 2024],
100 chose the dose on a second set and scored a third with both judges (Appendix C). The
101 obscure-minus-popular direction raised Llama's abstention from 0.01 to 0.10 (+0.09 [+0.06,
102 +0.12] over random directions, short of the 0.10 bar fixed in advance; +0.17 with a majority
103 of three judges) and Gemma's from 0.00 to 0.04 at 4× its natural norm (Figure 2a). In
104 Llama this direction is nearly parallel to the abstention direction (cosine 0.95), and with
105 that component removed it adds +0.01 [-0.00, +0.02] (+0.02 with three judges; Gemma

109 Base models. The Jacobian lens maps a layer's activations to scores over output tokens
110 [Gurnee et al., 2026]; with its prefitted lenses we read a fixed set of hedge tokens against
111 the answer token in four base models of three families, at a forced-answer position. In the
112 mid-depth window fixed before the run, the obscure-minus-popular effect is positive in all
113 four models, and it sits in the hedge-token term itself rather than only in how rare the
114 answer token is (Figure 3b; all four intervals exclude zero). But the knowledge effect on
115 that term is about as large (Figure 3b), and on the full readout OLMo's knowledge effect
116 is negative (Figure 3a; Appendix D). A popularity-sensitive hedge signal thus exists before
117 instruction tuning, while Llama-Instruct's preference for popularity over knowledge is not
118 seen in base models. The human-memory prediction that familiarity acts at a shallower
119 depth than knowledge [Koriat and Levy-Sadot, 2001] failed (1 supporting, 1 contradicting, 6
120 undecided of eight models; Appendix D).

4 121 Related work

122 Entity-recognition features that gate refusal carry over from base to chat models [Ferrando
123 et al., 2025]; Lindsey et al. [2025] describe the misfire hypothesis we test. Brzezinka [2026]
124 separates graded familiarity probes from the abstention policy, without a knowledge axis.
125 Models threshold internal confidence when abstention is offered in a multiple-choice prompt
126 [Kumaran et al., 2026]; ours is uninstructed and free-form. Confidence also tracks how
127 popular an answer is [Ni et al., 2025], and verbal and semantic uncertainty diverge [Ji et al.,
128 2025]; we add what each follows, and that it differs across families.

5 129 Limitations and conclusion

130 Abstention is judged by LLMs not validated against human labels, and the popularity
131 preference is shown for one model; Gemma lacks it. Popularity is a page-view proxy, models
132 have 2­9B parameters, and questions are single-entity PopQA questions. Neither steering
133 nor the lens isolates a familiarity cause.

134 In Llama, answer uncertainty follows knowledge and abstention follows popularity, so
135 predicting abstention from answer uncertainty misses its main driver; calibration work
136 should measure both. That only the first holds in every family suggests the two are shaped
137 separately.

G 488 Timing, neurons and the circuit

489 On matched pairs of obscure and popular questions in Llama, the lens-decoded abstention
490 readout reaches half its final size at layer 16, while the entropy gap does so at layer 28,
491 12 layers later; this compares two readouts of one contrast, not the onsets in Appendix D.
492 Neurons selected for their correlation with output entropy in Llama and Qwen behaved as
493 word-frequency and temperature neurons [Stolfo et al., 2024, Gurnee et al., 2024]. A set of
494 20 attention heads and 100 MLP neurons found by activation patching [Wang et al., 2023,
495 Syed et al., 2024] reproduces 0.83 [0.78, 0.87] of the first-token abstention log-odds difference
496 between matched pairs; in generation, removing its state removes abstention in 0.98 of cases
497 but injecting it produces abstention in only 0.40, short of a behavioural bar fixed in advance.
498 Shared uncertainty circuits [Teplica et al., 2025], a commit-or-abstain circuit across ten
499 models [Nguyen et al., 2026] and a similar asymmetry between releasing and restoring refusal
500 [Liu et al., 2026] have been reported before.
