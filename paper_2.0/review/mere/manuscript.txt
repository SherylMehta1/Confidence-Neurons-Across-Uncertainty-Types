1 In Llama, Saying "I Don't Know" Tracks Entity Popularity More Than Knowledge

2

3 ANONYMOUS AUTHOR(S)

4

5   When a language model says "I don't know", we would like that to mean it lacks the fact. We test this in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct by

6   crossing entity popularity (top vs. bottom decile of Wikipedia page views in PopQA) with knowledge labels taken from the model's

7

         own forced answers. Take facts Llama knows (its greedy answer and at least 9 of 10 sampled answers are correct): it says "I don't
8

         know" in 43% of replies when the entity is obscure, and in 0% when it is popular. Saying "I don't know" tracks popularity more than

9

10  knowledge, by +0.30 [+0.20, +0.40]. We then test how far this holds. It replicates with fresh replies (+0.29) and in new questions

11  (+0.10), and survives removing facts recoverable from the name alone (+0.36). It also weakens: its size depends on the abstention

12 measure (+0.13 to +0.44), all label corrections at once leave +0.14 [-0.01, +0.28], and resampling relations gives [-0.10, +0.43], because

13 80 of 87 obscure known facts come from three relations. The firmest result is narrower: among facts Llama does not know, it abstains

14  more on obscure entities (+0.34 [+0.25, +0.43]; +0.18 [+0.04, +0.31] after all corrections). Gemma-2-9B shows the same preference

15  when the prompt permits "I don't know"; for three other families the judges disagree too much to measure it. We could not show that

16  familiarity causes this: steering and lens readouts point to a generic abstention signal, and no model-side measure shows the obscure

17

18  names to be unfamiliar. In this model, "I don't know" is not a plain report of a missing fact.

26 1 Introduction

27 Saying "I don't know" when a fact is missing avoids the worst hallucinations [12]. To trust that statement, we need to

28

        know what it responds to. Confidence estimates usually read uncertainty over answers [11, 15, 24], and models use

29

30 such confidence when a prompt offers abstention [16]. However, verbal and answer-level uncertainty diverge [9], so

31 what a model's "I don't know" actually tracks is less clear.

32  One candidate is familiarity. In people, the sense of being able to answer depends on how familiar a question's terms

33

        are as well as on whether the answer can be retrieved [14, 21, 23]. In language models, "known entity" features suppress

34

35 a default "can't answer" response. Lindsey et al. [17] propose that a familiar name can trigger these features when the

36 fact is missing, so the model answers when it should abstain. We call this a misfire. The converse failure is an unfamiliar

37 name that does not suppress the default: the model then abstains on a fact it knows.

38

            Testing this requires separating familiarity from knowledge. Existing tests define an entity as known when the model

39

40 answers about it correctly [5], so the two move together. Familiarity has been separated from factual reliability in

41 representations [2] and shown to shape hallucination through fine-tuning data [13], but, to our knowledge, not crossed

42 with knowledge in abstention behavior. We use page-view popularity as the measurable stand-in for familiarity, and we

43

        cross it with knowledge. If abstention follows popularity, two cells show it: popular unknown facts should draw few "I

44

45 don't know" replies (misfires), and obscure known facts many (the default not suppressed).

46  In Llama, they do. The rest of this paper follows that result. We show how we separate the two factors (Section 2) and

47 what Llama does (Section 3). We then ask whether the gap holds (Section 4), whether other models share it (Section 5),

48

        and whether familiarity causes it (Section 6).

63 2 Crossing Popularity With Knowledge

64

65 To separate the two factors, each question needs a popularity level and a knowledge label that are set independently,

66 and abstention must be measured apart from both.

67

68      Questions & Labels. We use the 16 relations of PopQA [18]. Within each relation, subjects in the bottom and top

69 deciles of Wikipedia page views form the obscure and popular arms. Knowledge comes from the model's own answers

70

71 [6]: we grade a greedy answer and ten samples ( = 0.7) against gold aliases, under a forced-answer prompt (the chat

72 template plus the assistant prefix "The answer is"). A fact is known if the greedy answer and at least 9 samples are correct,

73 and unknown if neither the greedy answer nor any sample is; the band between is dropped. "Known" is thus specific

74 to this prompt. This leaves 2,303, 2,392, 2,282 and 2,378 questions for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct,

75

76 Gemma-2-9B-it and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [7, 10, 20, 25] (cell counts in Appendix A).

77

78      Two Readouts. Answer uncertainty is the first-token entropy under the forced-answer prompt. The knowledge labels

79 come from the same distribution, so entropy should follow knowledge; it serves as a control. Abstention is measured

80 with a free prompt (the chat template alone): for up to 100 questions per popularity × knowledge cell (387 in total), we

81

        draw three replies ( = 0.7, up to 96 tokens). Two LLM judges (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, Gemma-2-27B-it) label each reply

82

83 DONT_KNOW, CLARIFY (asks which entity is meant) or ANSWER; a reply abstains when both say DONT_KNOW. On

84 Llama the judges agree at Cohen's  = 0.62 [0.57, 0.67], above a pre-specified bar of 0.60 for the point estimate; on the

85 other families they do not.

86

87

            Effects. The popularity effect is the obscure-minus-popular difference in a readout, averaged over the two knowledge

88

89 levels; the knowledge effect is the unknown-minus-known difference, averaged over popularity. Their difference is

90 the model's popularity preference: positive when abstention follows popularity more than knowledge. In this equally

91 weighted 2 × 2, it equals abstention on obscure known facts minus abstention on popular unknown facts. Intervals

92

        are 95% bootstraps over questions within cells unless stated; because arms are defined within relations, we also report

93

94 relation-clustered intervals. Unless marked post hoc, every analysis followed a plan written before it ran (pre-specified);

95 the plans are self-recorded, not externally timestamped.

213 5 Gemma Shares the Preference When Permitted

214  Plain Prompt: Too Few Abstentions to Measure. Entropy follows knowledge in all four families (Figure 1c), but Qwen,

215 Gemma and Mistral rarely say "I don't know"; Gemma abstains in at most 0.11 of replies per cell, so its preference

216

217 cannot be estimated. A pre-specified four-family test found partial support for Qwen and Mistral (only the entropy

218 condition holds), and full support for Gemma on a readout that shows no preference in Llama (Appendix B).

219

220  Permissive Prompt: Same Preference in Gemma. To obtain enough abstentions, we ended each question with "If you

221 do not know the answer, say that you do not know." Llama and Gemma then abstain often and three judges agree
222 (Fleiss  = 0.71 and 0.67). The popularity preference is +0.47 [+0.35, +0.59] for Llama and +0.30 [+0.19, +0.40] for

223

224 Gemma, which says "I don't know" to 0.41 of obscure known and 0.00 of popular known questions. For Qwen, Mistral

225 and OLMo-2-7B, the judges disagree (Fleiss  0.40­0.50), so their preferences are unmeasured (point estimates +0.15,

226 +0.16, -0.09; Appendix B).

227  The preference therefore appears in the two families where we can measure it, and we cannot say more about the

228

229 others. Two models sharing a behavior does not tell us why it happens. If the cause is familiarity, the model should

230 represent obscure names as unfamiliar, and a popularity direction should do something an abstention direction does

231 not. We test both.

        6 No Familiarity Mechanism Isolated

234

235  Obscure Names Do Not Look Unfamiliar to Llama. Entity recognition, an LLM-graded test of whether Llama can

236 describe the entity, averages 0.83 for obscure known facts and 0.75 for popular known ones, and shrinks the popularity

237

        coefficient by 5%. Wikidata name collisions (post hoc) leave it at +0.34 [+0.27, +0.41]. Mean name surprisal under base

238

239 Llama is uncorrelated with log page views (Spearman -0.001), so it does not stand in for popularity. Name form explains

240 part of the gap: between 55 matched pairs of known facts, the regex abstention gap of +0.76 shrinks to +0.43 [+0.18,

241 +0.69] once the names' word frequency, token count and token rarity are held equal (post hoc).

242

243  Steering Finds a Generic Abstention Signal. Like work steering refusal along recognition directions [3, 5], we fitted

244

        obscure-minus-popular, unknown-minus-known and abstain-minus-answer directions on half of the questions [1, 22],

245

246 chose the dose on a second set of popular known questions and scored a third (Figure 2, Appendix C). In Llama,

247 obscure-minus-popular adds +0.09 [+0.06, +0.12] abstention over random directions (short of the pre-specified +0.10

248 bar). It is nearly parallel to abstention directions fitted without any popularity contrast: cosine 0.91 for one fitted on

249

        obscure questions only and 0.89 for one fitted on popular questions only. Removing either leaves +0.01 [+0.00, +0.03]

250

251 and +0.01 [-0.00, +0.02]. It beats unknown-minus-known on abstention (+0.06), as the cells' rates predict; in Gemma,

252 unknown-minus-known raises entropy +0.66 [+0.58, +0.74] nats more (Figure 2b; cf. [4]). The popularity direction

253 therefore moves abstention without showing a popularity-specific route.

254

256

257

258 and OLMo-3-7B [19], and knowledge raises it comparably or more in three of four (Table 3). Instruct partners lean

259 further toward knowledge, so this readout does not show where Llama-Instruct's preference arises. The pre-specified

283 7 Limitations

284

285 Abstention is judged by LLMs not validated against human labels; their errors could differ by cell and could bias the

286 preference in either direction. "Known" is specific to the forced-answer prompt and concentrated in three relations.

287 The design contrasts extreme deciles, and the adjacent bands give a smaller preference. Popularity is a stand-in that

288

        also carries name form. The models have 2­9B parameters, and only two families could be measured. Hedging driven

289

290 by ambiguity that the judges do not label as clarification is not excluded.

291

292 8 Conclusion

293

        We began by asking what a model's "I don't know" responds to. In Llama, on these questions, it responds to how popular

294

295 the entity is more than to whether the model can give the answer: 0.43 on obscure facts it knows, 0.13 on popular facts

296 it does not. The comparison between the two factors is suggestive rather than settled, since it shrinks to +0.14 [-0.01,

297 +0.28] under joint label corrections and rests on three relations. The popularity gap among unknown facts is firmer.

298

        Gemma shows the same preference when the prompt permits abstaining.

299

300     Interpretation: the result has the shape the misfire idea predicts, but we did not establish its mechanism. Our measures

301 do not show obscure names to be unfamiliar, and steering isolates only a generic abstention signal. Surface features of

302 names and instruction tuning remain open alternatives, and more investigation is needed.

303

            Recommendation: an evaluation that reads abstention as a measure of what a model does not know should cross it

304

305 with entity popularity and report per-cell rates. Here, a single abstention rate would hide that the same statement is

306 common on obscure facts the model knows and rare on popular facts it does not.
